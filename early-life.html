Early Life and Education

Education became an important part of Funmilayo's life from an early age. From 1906 to 1913, she attended St. John's Primary School in Abeokuta before entering Abeokuta Grammar School in 1914. She was among the first girls admitted to the school, and is widely identified as its first female student. At a time when formal education for Nigerian girls was far from common, her presence in the classroom was significant. It represented a step beyond the educational opportunities traditionally available to many girls of her time. Her admission to Abeokuta Grammar School marked an important stage in her development. Education offered her more than academic knowledge; it provided opportunities to engage with ideas and develop the confidence and abilities that would serve her in later life. Her experience also formed part of a wider period of change, as access to formal schooling gradually opened new possibilities for some Nigerian girls and women. Her academic journey continued beyond Abeokuta. In 1919, she travelled to England to study at Wincham Hall School for Girls in Cheshire. There, she studied subjects including music, French, domestic science and elocution. Living and studying in a different country exposed her to new experiences, customs and ways of life. It also brought her face to face with racial prejudice, according to biographical accounts, giving her personal insight into the inequalities associated with race and colonial society. Her time in England was significant not only for the education she received but also for her growing sense of identity. During this period, she chose to leave behind the Christian names Frances and Abigail and increasingly used her Yoruba name, Funmilayo. This decision reflected a stronger identification with her African heritage at a time when colonial influence shaped many aspects of Nigerian life. She returned to Nigeria in 1922, bringing her education and experiences abroad back to the community in which she had grown up. Rather than remaining overseas, she returned to Abeokuta, where she would put her education to practical use. She subsequently worked as a teacher at Abeokuta Grammar School, beginning a career that connected her directly with young people and the wider community. Teaching became an important foundation for her later work. Through education, she could contribute to the development of others and engage with the challenges facing the society around her. Although her later activism would take her far beyond the classroom, these early experiences formed part of the journey through which she developed into a prominent advocate for women's rights and social change. Her early life and education therefore marked more than a series of schools and qualifications. They brought her into contact with different societies, shaped her understanding of identity and inequality, and prepared her for a life of public engagement. The classroom was one of the earliest settings in which she would contribute to her community, long before her activism brought her into the national spotlight.
